## What this skill does

An upgrade over "search around and summarize" for research that actually matters: market research, competitor analysis, prep before a big meeting or decision. Look at a topic through several distinct perspectives before writing anything, instead of one pass of undifferentiated searching.

## Execution

### Step 1: Pick the lenses

For the topic at hand, name 4-5 distinct expert perspectives that would each notice something different. Don't default to a fixed list — pick lenses that actually fit the topic. Examples of the shape (adapt per topic, don't reuse blindly):

- **The practitioner** — someone doing this work day to day. What's actually true on the ground, not in theory?
- **The skeptic** — what's the strongest case this doesn't work, or that the conventional wisdom here is wrong?
- **The numbers person** — what does the data actually say? Real figures, not vibes.
- **The historian** — what's been tried before, and what happened?
- **The outsider** — someone from a completely different field looking at this fresh — what's obvious to them that insiders have stopped seeing?

### Step 2: Research each lens separately

Run research (web search, internal docs, whatever's relevant) through each lens as its own question, not one blended search. Keep each lens's findings distinct before combining them.

### Step 3: Map the contradictions

Before writing the synthesis, explicitly name where the lenses disagree. Contradictions are often the most useful output of a real research pass — a synthesis that smooths them over quietly is worse than one that flags them.

### Step 4: Synthesize a briefing

Write the actual output — a short, structured briefing, not a wall of undifferentiated notes. Lead with the answer, back it with the lens findings, name the open contradictions and what would resolve them.

### Step 5: Adversarial review

Before calling it done, read the synthesis back looking for the weakest claim in it — the one an informed skeptic would push back on hardest. Either strengthen it with better evidence or flag it explicitly as the shakiest part of the briefing. Check that every load-bearing claim traces back to something from Step 2, not something that crept in during synthesis unsupported.

## When to use it

Meeting prep, market validation, competitor moves, "what should our position be on X" — anything where getting it wrong costs a real decision. Not for quick factual lookups.
